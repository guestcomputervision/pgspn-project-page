From Ideal Transmission to Learned Surrogate

PGSPN converts learned per-channel transmission maps into a metric-scale depth prior via an affine mapping in the log-domain (Eqs. (6)–(9) of the main paper). This section provides the complete derivation of why an affine mapping—with both scale and offset—is the physically correct choice, rather than the zero-intercept scaling that the ideal Beer–Lambert law would suggest.

Step 1: The Ideal Beer–Lambert Relation and Why It Breaks

Under the standard Beer–Lambert law, the physical transmission $\mathbf{T}_{\text{phys},c}(\mathbf{x})$ along channel $c$ and the metric depth $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x})$ satisfy

$$\mathbf{T}_{\text{phys},c}(\mathbf{x}) = \exp\!\big(-\beta_c \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x})\big), \qquad \log \mathbf{T}_{\text{phys},c}(\mathbf{x}) = -\beta_c \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x}). \tag{S1}$$

In this ideal model, $-\log \mathbf{T}_{\text{phys},c}(\mathbf{x})$ is a zero-intercept linear function of $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x})$. In practice, however, real underwater imaging deviates from this simplified setting: backscatter, spatially varying attenuation, sensor response, and nonlinear in-camera processing (e.g., gamma correction and tone mapping) all distort the observed intensities, so the simple zero-intercept relation between log-transmission and depth does not hold exactly.

Similar concerns have been raised in terrestrial dehazing. The classical atmospheric scattering model (ASM) accounts for only a single haze factor and fails to explain additional degradations such as light trapping, noise, and blur, which leads to a pronounced domain gap between ASM-synthesized and real hazy images. Feng et al. address this with a multi-degradation hazy imaging model:

$$\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}) \Big( A^{\gamma}\big(\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x})\, \mathbf{T}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}) + 1 - \mathbf{T}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}) \big) + \mathbf{N} \Big), \tag{S2}$$

in which the transmission term appears as $\mathbf{T}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{x})$ and is further scaled by $A^{\gamma}$. Thus, even if the underlying physical transmission obeys Beer–Lambert, the effective transmission inside the imaging model is a power-law–distorted version of it.

Step 2: Modeling the Transmission Surrogate

A crucial observation is that the network never observes $\mathbf{T}_{\text{phys},c}(\mathbf{x})$ directly—it only sees RGB intensities after underwater optics, camera response, in-camera processing, and data augmentations. As a result, the network effectively learns a transmission surrogate $\hat{\mathbf{T}}_c(\mathbf{x})$ that plays the role of the transmission term in Eq. (S2), but is related to the true physical transmission only up to an unknown scale and nonlinearity. We model this surrogate as a simple but expressive generalization of the ideal law:

$$\hat{\mathbf{T}}_c(\mathbf{x}) \approx A_c \, \mathbf{T}_{\text{phys},c}(\mathbf{x})^{\gamma_c}, \tag{S3}$$

where $A_c \gt 0$ and $\gamma_c \gt 0$ are channel-wise constants absorbing unknown gains and nonlinearities. This power-law form is a minimal monotone generalization of the Beer–Lambert law that naturally arises from camera gamma curves, contrast adjustments, and the rescaling induced by the final activation and normalization layers of the network—and it has exactly the same structure as the transmission factor in Eq. (S2). The assumption $\gamma_c \gt 0$ ensures the surrogate preserves the monotonic relation between depth and transmission.

Substituting Eq. (S1) into Eq. (S3) gives

$$\hat{\mathbf{T}}_c(\mathbf{x}) \approx A_c \exp\!\big(-\gamma_c \beta_c \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x})\big) = A_c \exp\!\big(-\tilde{\beta}_c \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x})\big), \qquad \tilde{\beta}_c = \gamma_c \beta_c. \tag{S4}$$

For each channel $c \in \{r,g,b\}$, we work in the log-domain and define the depth-like quantity

$$s_c(\mathbf{x}) = -\log \hat{\mathbf{T}}_c(\mathbf{x}). \tag{S5}$$

Using Eq. (S4), we obtain

$$s_c(\mathbf{x}) = -\log \hat{\mathbf{T}}_c(\mathbf{x}) \approx -\log A_c + \tilde{\beta}_c\, \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x}) = \alpha_c\, \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x}) + c_{0,c}, \tag{S6}$$

where $\alpha_c = \tilde{\beta}_c \gt 0$ and $c_{0,c} = -\log A_c$. Eq. (S6) shows that, under the power-law surrogate model, the log-domain surrogate $s_c(\mathbf{x})$ and the metric depth $\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{x})$ are related by an affine mapping in general. The ideal Beer–Lambert case corresponds to $A_c = 1$ and $\gamma_c = 1$, for which $c_{0,c} = 0$ and the mapping reduces to a zero-intercept line. Once $A_c \neq 1$ or $\gamma_c \neq 1$, a non-zero offset $c_{0,c}$ naturally appears.

Step 3: Affine Calibration with Sparse Depth Extended derivation

To resolve this affine ambiguity in practice, we calibrate each channel-wise surrogate $s_c(\mathbf{x})$ using sparse metric depth measurements. Let $\{d_i\}$ denote sparse depth samples at pixel locations $\{\mathbf{x}_i\}$, and let $s_c(\mathbf{x}_i)$ be the corresponding log-surrogate values for channel $c$. For each channel, we fit an affine mapping

$$d_i \approx a_c\, s_c(\mathbf{x}_i) + b_c, \qquad c \in \{r,g,b\}, \tag{S7}$$

where $(a_c, b_c)$ are channel-specific scale and offset parameters, estimated by least squares:

$$(a_c^\ast, b_c^\ast) = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{a_c,\, b_c} \sum_{i} \big(a_c\, s_c(\mathbf{x}_i) + b_c - d_i\big)^2. \tag{S8}$$

By Eq. (S6), in the ideal case the fitted parameters $(a_c^\ast, b_c^\ast)$ recover the effective inverse scale and offset of the surrogate, $\big(1/\alpha_c,\, -c_{0,c}/\alpha_c\big)$, under real imaging conditions. In practice, each channel exhibits different attenuation behavior and noise characteristics, so the quality of the affine fit varies across channels. We therefore evaluate the reconstruction error of each calibrated channel and select the most reliable one:

$$c^\ast = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{c \in \{r,g,b\}} \sum_{i} \big(a_c^\ast\, s_c(\mathbf{x}_i) + b_c^\ast - d_i\big)^2. \tag{S9}$$

The final metric depth prior used in our framework is the calibrated surrogate of the best channel:

$$\hat{d}(\mathbf{x}) = a_{c^\ast}^\ast\, s_{c^\ast}(\mathbf{x}) + b_{c^\ast}^\ast. \tag{S10}$$

This selection strategy picks the channel least affected by absorption and scattering, providing a sparsity-robust, boundary-preserving initialization for the subsequent PGPS refinement. Eqs. (S7)–(S10) correspond to Eqs. (7)–(9) in Sec. IV-B of the main paper.

Why not force $b_c = 0$? Forcing a zero offset would incorrectly impose the idealized zero-intercept relation from Eq. (S1) directly on the learned surrogate, contradicting the generalized affine form of Eq. (S6). Empirically, constraining $b_c = 0$ consistently degrades performance, whereas full affine calibration yields a more stable and accurate metric depth prior—confirming that real underwater data and the learned transmission surrogate do not strictly follow a zero-intercept model, and that the affine depth conversion used in the main paper is both physically plausible and practically necessary.

Analysis of Underwater Sparse Depth Distribution

Why is underwater depth completion fundamentally harder than its terrestrial counterpart? To characterize the data regime, we analyze the spatial distribution of valid depth in terrestrial (KITTI) and underwater (FLSea-Stereo, FLSea-VI) datasets. While KITTI provides relatively dense and uniformly distributed valid depth, underwater scenes exhibit large measurement-free regions associated with open water or degraded visibility.

Qualitative comparison of depth-support regions. (a) Sample from KITTI; (b)–(c) samples from FLSea-Stereo and FLSea-VI. Underwater scenes contain extensive unsupervised regions, often corresponding to open water or visually degraded areas.

We quantify point density via a grid-occupancy measure: each depth map is partitioned into $32 \times 32$ and $64 \times 64$ grids, and a cell counts as occupied if it contains at least one valid depth sample. With $N_{\text{grid}}$ total cells and $N_{\text{occ}}$ occupied cells, the Occupied Grids Ratio (OGR) is

$$\mathrm{OGR} = \frac{N_{\text{occ}}}{N_{\text{grid}}}.$$

Computed per frame and averaged over each sequence, underwater datasets exhibit markedly lower OGR than KITTI (32×32 grid: 86.32% for KITTI vs. 50.20% / 47.52% for FLSea-Stereo / FLSea-VI; 64×64 grid: 92.03% vs. 53.98% / 51.23%), indicating large contiguous regions without depth support—especially at the finer grid resolution. This sparsity pattern underlies depth bleeding and motivates the physics-guided priors in PGSPN.

Grid-wise occupied depth ratios across datasets. Fraction of grid cells containing at least one valid depth point for (a) $32 \times 32$ and (b) $64 \times 64$ partitions. Underwater datasets show markedly lower occupancy than KITTI, highlighting the prevalence of large supervision-free regions.

Depth Bleeding Index: Per-Sample Analysis & Joint Interpretation

The DBI definition, synthetic open-water generation, and reliability analysis against real open-water regions are given in Sec. V-A of the main paper. Here we complement them with per-sample examples and discuss how DBI should be read together with the standard pointwise accuracy metrics.

Per-Sample Examples

Per-sample DBI values align with the aggregate trends in the main paper: PGSPN consistently achieves the lowest DBI (0.254, 0.432), indicating well-preserved discontinuities at open-water boundaries, while BP-Net exhibits the highest DBI (0.781, 0.621) with visible bleeding into the synthetic open-water region; DySPN falls in between (0.511, 0.551). This confirms that DBI meaningfully captures the severity of bleeding artifacts at the individual image level.

Qualitative DBI examples under SfM 100% input. Lower DBI indicates better-preserved depth discontinuities at open-water boundaries.

Reading DBI Together with Pointwise Accuracy

DBI measures depth discontinuity preservation at open-water boundaries—by design, it does not directly reflect pointwise depth accuracy within the evaluated regions. For instance, a method producing sharp but biased predictions at boundaries may still achieve a favorable DBI score, and DBI does not exclusively isolate bleeding from other discontinuity sources. This is why, in all experiments, we jointly report DBI alongside standard metrics (MAE, RMSE, $\delta_k$) computed over GT-valid regions: the two views are complementary, and bleeding suppression should always be interpreted in the context of overall depth accuracy. A method is only convincing when it scores well on both—as PGSPN does across all sequences and sparsity conditions.

Since open-water regions inherently lack ground-truth depth, directly measuring bleeding against ground truth is impossible; DBI offers a practical alternative using reliable boundary predictions as references. We hope this metric, interpreted jointly with pointwise accuracy, provides a useful baseline for future work on evaluating depth completion in supervision-free regions.

Framework-Level Contributions

PGSPN deliberately reuses proven architectures—AdaBins for transmission estimation and LRRU for depth refinement—with minimal modifications. A natural question is whether the observed improvements stem from these architectural components or from our framework-level design: the two-stage training strategy, photometric supervision, transmission-based depth conversion, and physics-guided propagation suppression. Two pieces of evidence show the contribution lies in the latter.

TRUDepth vs. PGSPN-prior. TRUDepth is a monocular depth-regression baseline built on the same AdaBins backbone as our Stage 1 network, differing only in output-layer dimensionality. Yet PGSPN-prior consistently outperforms TRUDepth across all sequences and sparsity conditions—e.g., on rock_garden1 under SfM 100%, MAE decreases from 0.236 m to 0.164 m and DBI from 0.296 to 0.211. Because the backbone is shared, this gap arises entirely from the training strategy: replacing monocular depth regression with per-channel transmission estimation, photometric restoration loss, and affine depth conversion. The architectural modification is minimal; the methodological change is decisive.

LRRU vs. LRRU + our prior. The vanilla LRRU baseline, initialized from sparse depth alone, achieves MAE 0.219 and DBI 0.724 on rock_garden1. Simply replacing this initialization with our transmission-based prior—without any change to the LRRU architecture or loss—reduces MAE to 0.090 ($2.43\times$ improvement) and DBI to 0.258 ($\downarrow$64.3%). LRRU's severe degradation under underwater sparsity is not an architectural limitation but an initialization problem that the physics-guided prior directly resolves.

By formulating underwater depth completion as a two-stage physics-guided pipeline with photometric supervision, we transform existing architectures into systems that robustly handle irregular sparsity and depth bleeding. This architecture-agnostic design also means future backbone improvements can be readily integrated into the framework.

Efficiency and Turbidity Analysis

Computational Cost

We analyze per-module cost on an RTX 3090 Ti and NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin at 640×384 resolution. The transmission estimation stage (29.76M parameters) accounts for the majority of inference time as it processes full-resolution images through an encoder–decoder network—but it is indispensable, since replacing LRRU's sparse-only initialization with our prior reduces MAE from 0.219 to 0.090 and DBI by 64.3%. The depth refinement stage (17.96M + 2.89M parameters) adds modest overhead, and PGPS itself requires only element-wise operations on the affinity weights, contributing negligible cost (0.6 ms desktop, 4.2 ms AGX Orin) for an additional 18.2% DBI reduction.

For deployment feasibility, we benchmark on Jetson AGX Orin under progressive optimization: FP32 runs at 2.6 Hz, mixed-precision (AMP) at 4.1 Hz, torch.compile at 3.6 Hz, both combined at 6.3 Hz, and TensorRT (FP16) reaches 8.1 Hz—sufficient for keyframe-based underwater mapping and obstacle avoidance where robots typically operate at 0.5–2 m/s and process every 3rd–5th frame.

Impact of Water Turbidity

Our framework leverages optical attenuation as a depth cue—so how does performance vary with water clarity? We evaluate on the Joint-ID synthetic dataset, which provides controlled turbidity via Jerlov water types from 5C (highly turbid) to I (near-clear) with corresponding attenuation coefficients $(\beta_r, \beta_g, \beta_b)$. PGSPN's advantage is strongest under high turbidity (types 5C–1C), where wavelength-dependent attenuation produces rich photometric cues for the transmission network. As turbidity decreases toward clear water (types IB–I), the attenuation signal weakens and the gap narrows—but PGSPN never falls behind: it remains competitive with the best baselines (DySPN, TWISE), because the depth completion backbone retains effectiveness regardless of the photometric prior's informativeness. Reduced turbidity diminishes our advantage without introducing any disadvantage, making PGSPN well-suited for the turbid conditions common in real underwater operations.

Robustness Analysis

Complementing the limitation analysis in the main paper (Sec. V-E), we examine two robustness aspects that could not be covered there: behavior under real sensor noise, and an optional strategy for rejecting gross outliers in the sparse depth used for affine calibration.

Robustness to Sparse Depth Noise (Real Sonar)

Our main experiments sample sparse depth from ground truth to isolate sparsity patterns from sensor noise. To examine behavior under real sensor noise, we evaluate on sonar-RGB imagery where sparse depth comes directly from sonar measurements. PGSPN produces more coherent depth maps than baselines under noisy sparse inputs, since the transmission-based prior is derived entirely from RGB observations and is therefore unaffected by sparse depth noise. We note the noise level in this dataset is relatively mild: systematic evaluation under severe measurement noise (multi-path reflections, gross triangulation failures) and with diverse real sensor modalities remains future work—our Bias configuration captures the spatial distribution of FoV-limited sensors such as sonar, but not their sensor-specific noise properties.

Evaluation under real sensor noise. Qualitative depth completion on sonar-RGB imagery with real measurement noise. PGSPN produces more coherent results than TWISE, DySPN, and BP-Net.

Additional Strategy: Robust Rejection of Sparse Depth Outliers

PGSPN calibrates its transmission-derived pseudo-depth to metric scale by least-squares fitting a per-channel affine map to the sparse measurements (Eqs. (6)–(9) of the main paper). Because the main-paper experiments sample sparse depth from ground truth, gross outliers do not arise and the plain least-squares fit suffices. In real-sensor deployment, however, stereo, SfM, or sonar measurements can carry gross outliers, and since least squares minimizes squared residuals, even a handful can skew the global scale and offset and corrupt the entire dense prior. For such conditions one could enable an optional robust-calibration step—a toggleable component in our implementation, off by default and not part of the main-paper pipeline.

An optional robust fit. Starting from the least-squares estimate, the step would iteratively reject outliers with a median-absolute-deviation (MAD) criterion: keep sparse points whose residual lies within $\tau$ robust standard deviations of the median residual (a small MAD floor prevents the inlier band from collapsing), then re-fit the affine map on the survivors. The figure sweeps the threshold $\tau$ under 5% / 10% / 15% synthetic outlier ratios: outlier-detection F1 peaks at tighter thresholds, while the final depth RMSE stays low and nearly flat across a wide range of $\tau$—so the completed depth is largely insensitive to the exact threshold. We use $\tau = 1.0$, which removes the bulk of gross outliers while staying in this stable regime. Because the MAD test assumes an inlier majority, it weakens under very high or spatially clustered outlier fractions; robustly handling severe, structured measurement noise thus remains future work, consistent with the sensor-noise limitation above.

(a) Outlier-detection F1 and depth RMSE as the MAD rejection threshold $\tau$ varies, for 5% / 10% / 15% injected outlier ratios—RMSE is stable across a wide threshold range while F1 peaks at tighter thresholds.
